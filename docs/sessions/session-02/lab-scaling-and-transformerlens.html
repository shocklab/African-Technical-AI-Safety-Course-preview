Lab: scaling laws & a first look inside a model

Fit a power law, explore the compute trends, and open up GPT-2 in TransformerLens

What we'll cover

The term's first lab does three things: it gets your toolchain working on Colab, it makes the scaling laws from 2.3 tangible by fitting one yourself, and it gives you a first hands-on look inside a real transformer using TransformerLens, the library we'll lean on heavily for interpretability in Weeks 7–8.

Setup Colab — no GPU needed

Allow about 90 minutes. A Google account is the only prerequisite: everything runs on the free tier, and a GPU runtime only makes part 3 quicker.

  1. Open the starter notebook in Colab. It opens read-only from GitHub, so use File → Save a copy in Drive before you change anything; the copy in your Drive is the one you edit and submit.
  2. Run the first cell. It installs TransformerLens, which takes about a minute, and prints Colab: True when it has finished.
  3. Work down the notebook with Shift+Enter, reading each cell before you run it.

Prefer to build it yourself? That is the better exercise. Start a blank notebook, run !pip install transformer_lens, and follow the three parts below; the starter notebook is then a worked answer to check against. You can also read the whole notebook on this site or download the .ipynb for Jupyter.

The notebook runs as it stands. What you supply are the four cells marked "Your turn", which is where the thinking lives; the code is there to give you something to think about.

① Fit a scaling law

From data to a power-law exponent

Session 2.3 gave you the law. Here you recover its exponent from points, which is the only way to feel how small that exponent is.

  1. Get a set of (training compute \(C\), final loss \(L\)) points. The notebook generates them from Kaplan's published law, and says so in the code: they are not measured runs, so this step tests your fitting, not the law. If a CSV of real runs is distributed in class, set CSV_PATH to it and everything downstream is unchanged.
  2. Plot \(L\) against \(C\) on log–log axes. A power law is a straight line on these axes and on no others, so this plot is the check that a power law is the right model at all.
  3. Fit \(\log L = a - \alpha \log C\) by least squares and read off \(\alpha\).
  4. Extrapolate one order of magnitude past your largest point.

You should get \(\alpha \approx 0.05\), matching the \(\alpha_C^{\min} \approx 0.050\) Kaplan et al. report. If your value is far from that, the usual cause is fitting \(L\) against \(C\) rather than their logarithms. Sanity check on the size of the exponent: a tenfold increase in compute multiplies the loss by \(10^{-\alpha} \approx 0.89\), which is an improvement of roughly 11%. Tenfold, for 11%.

Write down: one sentence on why you should distrust your own extrapolation. Session 2.4 is where to look: what does a smooth loss curve fail to tell you about the capabilities that appear along it, and what happened when Chinchilla re-ran Kaplan's compute-optimal experiment more carefully?

② Explore the compute trends

Doubling time from Epoch AI data

A second measurement, this time on real data you did not collect, which brings its own problems.

  1. Load Epoch AI's notable-models dataset. The notebook reads the CSV straight from the URL; if that fails, the page has a "Download the data in CSV" button and you can upload the file to your session instead. The columns you need are Publication date and Training compute (FLOP).
  2. Keep the rows that have both, and restrict to models published from 2010 onwards. Roughly half the dataset has no compute estimate, which is itself worth noticing.
  3. Plot compute against date with a logarithmic y-axis, fit a straight line to \(\log_{10} C\) against time, and convert the slope to a doubling time.
  4. Re-run with the window starting in 2018 and again in 2020, and watch the answer move.

From 2010 you should get a doubling time of roughly 6 months, about 4x per year. Later windows give shorter times. Both are defensible; that they differ is the point.

Write down: two sentences. First, which quantity you actually measured, stated precisely: not "AI progress" but the thing on the y-axis and how it was estimated. Second, over what window, and what would change your answer. This is the Session 1.4 reader's checklist turned on a plot you made yourself, which is the harder case.

③ Open up a transformer

GPT-2 small in TransformerLens

Your first contact with the residual-stream picture from 2.2, on a real model: 12 layers, 12 heads, \(d_{\text{model}} = 768\). Loading the weights takes a minute or two and about 500 MB.

from transformer_lens import HookedTransformer
model = HookedTransformer.from_pretrained("gpt2")
logits = model("The Eiffel Tower is in the city of")
# inspect the next-token distribution
  1. Feed the model several prompts and read off the top five predicted next tokens with their probabilities. Ask where it is confident and where it is not, and keep confidence separate from correctness. On the Eiffel Tower prompt, GPT-2 small puts " London" slightly above " Paris": confidently wrong is a state these systems are perfectly capable of, and it is the whole reason 1.4 hands you a checklist.
  2. Capture activations with run_with_cache and print the shape of the residual stream. You should see (1, 11, 768) for that prompt: [batch, position, d_model], which is exactly the \(T \times d\) matrix from 2.2, one row per token. Print an attention pattern too, shaped [batch, head, query_pos, key_pos].
  3. Tokenise parallel English and isiZulu text with model.to_str_tokens and count the tokens in each. Use genuinely parallel sentences, the same meaning in both languages, or the comparison means nothing; the notebook pulls them from Masakhane's MAFAND-MT dataset. Average over a hundred or so pairs rather than trusting one sentence, and check tokens per character as well as tokens per sentence, so you know you are not simply measuring longer words.

Expect isiZulu to cost about 2.2x the tokens of the same meaning in English, and watch how the isiZulu words shatter into fragments: UKhomishana becomes ['UK', 'hom', 'ish', 'ana']. Across languages the notebook finds roughly 2x for Swahili, isiXhosa and isiZulu, 2.5x for Hausa, and over 4x for Yoruba, whose diacritics are especially expensive. GPT-2's vocabulary of 50,257 tokens was fitted mostly to English text, and this is what that decision costs everyone else.

Write down: a short paragraph on what your ratio implies, covering cost (APIs bill per token and context windows are counted in tokens), quality (the model sees the language in smaller, less meaningful pieces), and safety (if a language is under-represented enough to tokenise badly, what does that predict about how much safety training and red-teaming it received?). Sessions 9 and 18 return to this.

If something breaks

  • ModuleNotFoundError: transformer_lens after the install cell ran: Colab needs the runtime restarted occasionally after installing. Use Runtime → Restart session, then run the cells again from the top.
  • A deprecation warning about from_pretrained, or a note about unauthenticated Hugging Face requests: both are harmless. The model still loads.
  • The Epoch AI or MAFAND download fails: you are probably offline or behind a proxy. Download the file by hand and upload it to the session; the notebook says where each one comes from.
  • Everything is slow: check Runtime → Change runtime type. A GPU helps part 3 and nothing else, and the lab is designed to be fine without one.
